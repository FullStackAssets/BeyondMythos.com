Shaking up the old: reviving forgotten cocktail techniques

Dust off old methods and discover how a simple shake can transform familiar drinks.

Takeaway

A classic shaking technique can add texture and balance you didn’t know your cocktails needed.

What happened

While cleaning out a vintage bar guide, I tried a 19th‑century shake method on a modern whiskey sour. The result was a silkier foam and a subtler mouthfeel. It reminded me that old tricks still have power.

Why it matters

Home bartenders often rely on the same three shakes. Revisiting forgotten moves expands your toolset and keeps the ritual fresh. It also teaches you how technique shapes flavor perception.

How to think about it

Start with a low‑tech shake: no ice, just a dry shake to emulsify, then add ice for a second shake. Observe the foam and mouthfeel, and adjust timing by feel. Treat each shake as a micro‑experiment.

Pros
  • Richer texture
  • Enhanced aroma integration
  • Simple, no extra gear needed
Cons
  • Requires practice for timing
  • May over‑dilute if over‑shaken
  • Can be messy without a proper shaker
Watch out

Don’t shake too long or the drink can become watery.

FAQ

What’s a dry shake?

A shake without ice that creates foam by emulsifying egg whites or citrus oils.

How long should the first shake last?

Around 5‑7 seconds is enough to build foam without melting the ingredients.

Can I use this technique with non‑egg drinks?

Yes, it works well with citrus‑forward cocktails and those containing syrups that benefit from aeration.
