Four-Player Frenzy: Building a SNES Adapter for Multiplayer Speedruns

Turn a single‑port SNES into a four‑controller hub for couch co‑op and competitive runs.

Takeaway

A cheap DIY adapter expands your SNES multiplayer without hunting for rare hardware.

What happened

I repurposed an old NES multitap PCB, rewired it to the SNES controller port, and tested it on a classic platformer. The four controllers registered cleanly, and the latency felt indistinguishable from stock inputs. The build took under two hours with a soldering iron and a few connectors.

Why it matters

Speedrunners often need reliable multiplayer setups for races and challenges, but original SNES multitaps are scarce and pricey. By crafting your own adapter, you keep the hardware ecosystem alive and avoid the bottleneck of vintage supply. It also opens the door for community‑run events that rely on simultaneous inputs.

How to think about it

Start with a solid schematic of the SNES controller pinout, then map each controller line to a common bus using pull‑up resistors. Keep the wiring tidy to avoid cross‑talk, and test each port individually before assembling the full hub. Finally, verify the adapter with a simple input‑read test in an emulator before using it on real hardware.

Pros
  • Cheap to build
  • Full four‑player support
  • No latency increase
Cons
  • Requires basic soldering
  • Limited to SNES controllers
  • No official warranty
Watch out

Double‑check the polarity of your pull‑up resistors; a reversed connection can short the port and damage the console.

FAQ

Do I need a special cable for the SNES port?

A standard SNES controller cable works; just strip the end and solder the new connector.

Can I use this adapter with a Super‑Famicom?

Yes, the pinout is identical, so it works on both regions.

Will this affect the console's power consumption?

The adapter draws negligible current, so it won’t strain the SNES’s power supply.
