Micro-Deadpoints: Tiny Pauses, Huge Gains

A quick guide to using micro-deadpoints to boost control and power on indoor boulders.

Takeaway

A micro-deadpoint can turn a shaky move into a confident finish.

What happened

During a Friday night session, I hit a slab problem that kept slipping at the top. By deliberately holding a micro-deadpoint on the final foothold, I locked the move and sent it.

Why it matters

Micro-deadpoints train body awareness and reduce reliance on momentum, which translates to steadier climbing on any wall. They also teach you to read subtle holds that others overlook.

How to think about it

Identify the last foothold before a crux, pause for a split second, and shift weight onto it before committing. Practice this on easy routes, gradually reducing the pause as confidence grows.

Pros
  • Improves precision
  • Builds confidence on slab
  • Reduces slip-off risk
Cons
  • Can slow flow if overused
  • Requires good foot sensitivity
  • May feel awkward at first
Watch out

Don’t hold the deadpoint too long or you’ll waste energy and break the rhythm.

FAQ

What’s the difference between a deadpoint and a micro-deadpoint?

A deadpoint is a dynamic pause at the peak of a movement; a micro-deadpoint is a barely-noticeable split-second hold on a foothold.

Can I use micro-deadpoints on overhangs?

Yes, but keep the pause brief; on steep terrain the goal is still to maintain momentum.

How often should I train this technique?

Start with one micro-deadpoint per problem, then increase as it feels natural, aiming for a few minutes each session.
