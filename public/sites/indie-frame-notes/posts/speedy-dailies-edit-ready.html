Speedy dailies: turning raw footage into edit‑ready cuts in a day

A practical workflow to get daily footage organized and ready for edit before the day ends.

Takeaway

You can shave hours off post‑production by applying a disciplined, low‑tech dailies routine.

What happened

A small indie crew on a 3‑day shoot tried a streamlined dailies process: ingest, quick sync, and basic color‑lookup on the same laptop. By the end of each day they had a clean proxy folder ready for the editor. The result was a smoother edit timeline and less late‑night crunch.

Why it matters

Indie productions often bleed time and budget on post‑production bottlenecks. Getting dailies ready fast keeps momentum, reduces stress, and gives the director early feedback on coverage. It also helps catch technical issues before they become costly fixes.

How to think about it

Start with a single external SSD and a reliable ingest app; set a 30‑minute window to copy and verify footage. Apply a preset LUT for a quick visual reference, then rename files using a simple shot‑day‑take convention. Archive the raw files and keep only the proxies for the edit, freeing up storage and speeding up the timeline.

Pros
  • Faster feedback loop
  • Less data loss risk
  • Simplifies editing workflow
Cons
  • Requires disciplined timeboxing
  • Limited color grading depth at proxy stage
  • Initial setup takes a few minutes each day
Watch out

Don’t skip checksum verification; a corrupted file can slip through a rushed ingest and ruin a shot later.

FAQ

Do I need a powerful computer for this workflow?

A modest laptop with a fast SSD and enough RAM for proxy generation is sufficient; you’re not rendering full‑res footage.

Can I still do full‑resolution color grading later?

Yes—keep the original raws safely archived; the proxies are only for the edit, not the final grade.

What naming convention works best?

Use DAY‑SHOT‑TAKE (e.g., 01‑A‑001) to keep files sortable and instantly readable.
